# Research, gap analysis, and positioning

Read this when starting prep for a new company/role, or when the candidate wants their positioning or narrative sharpened.

## Full intake fields

Use these as a checklist, not a rigid form — gather what's available and flag what's missing rather than inventing it.

**Company**: name, website, careers/job page, product(s), industry, location, interview format, recruiter message(s), any other emails/messages from the company.

**Role**: exact job title, seniority, job description, required technologies, preferred technologies, responsibilities, team information.

**Candidate**: CV/resume, LinkedIn/profile, portfolio/GitHub, previous interview feedback, known weaknesses, career gaps, employment transitions, salary expectations (if relevant), availability, anything else relevant.

## Company research

Prioritize sources in roughly this order: official company website → official careers pages → official product pages → engineering/technology pages → company blog → leadership/team info → public employee profiles → interviews/podcasts/talks → job postings for the same or similar roles → reliable third-party sources → employee/community discussion (clearly flagged as opinion, not fact).

Research and summarize:
- What the company does, its business model, customers and customer segments
- Products, and how customers actually use them
- How the company makes money
- Competitive landscape and current strategic direction
- Important recent developments
- Company culture and, specifically, engineering culture

## Team research

Where findable:
- Team structure and responsibilities; how the engineering org is organized
- Product/design relationship to engineering
- Tech leadership, senior/staff/principal engineers, engineering managers, and relevant employees' backgrounds
- Technologies used, architecture, deployment/release practices, testing approach, cloud provider, observability, security posture, regulatory requirements, agile/product methodology

## Role research

Try to determine:
- What problem this hire is actually meant to solve
- What the team actually needs (vs. what the JD lists)
- What "senior" specifically means at this org
- Expected ownership, technical/product/communication/leadership expectations
- Likely first 30/60/90-day expectations
- Likely interview stages and evaluation criteria

Tag everything **Confirmed** / **Strong inference** / **Unknown**. Never present a guess as a fact.

## Employee / role benchmarking

Where public profiles are findable (current team, same role, comparable senior engineering roles elsewhere, former employees), look for patterns in years of experience, previous companies, technologies, education, domain experience, career progression, responsibilities, technical leadership, product ownership, communication style, and specialization.

The point is **not** to help the candidate imitate specific individuals. The point is to answer: *"What does this company appear to consider a successful senior engineer?"* — then compare that pattern against the candidate's actual CV.

## Gap analysis

Build a requirement-by-requirement table:

| Requirement | Candidate Evidence | Strength | Gap | Interview Strategy |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Classify each requirement as **Strong match**, **Good match**, **Transferable**, **Partial**, **Gap**, or **Unknown**.

For genuine gaps, never coach bluffing. Instead, build a version of:

> "I haven't done X extensively in production, but I have done A/B/C, which gives me a strong foundation, and this is how I'd ramp up."

## Candidate positioning

Build a concise thesis answering: *why should this company hire this candidate over another qualified senior engineer?*

Identify 3–5 memorable, CV-supported differentiators. Typical categories (only use ones the CV actually backs up): deep frontend + full-stack reach, product engineering instincts, end-to-end ownership, experience in regulated/high-trust environments, cloud/DevOps fluency, business/product background, technical leadership, cross-disciplinary range.

These differentiators should show up naturally throughout the interview prep — stories, "why us" answers, and questions for the interviewer should all reinforce the same thesis without it feeling like a rehearsed pitch.

## Core narrative

Build one coherent career story with four beats:

- **Past** — what have they built and learned?
- **Present** — what kind of engineer are they now?
- **Future** — where are they deliberately going?
- **Why this company** — why does this role logically fit that trajectory?

By the end of the interview, the interviewer should be able to summarize the candidate in one sentence, e.g.: *"Senior product-focused engineer with strong frontend depth, full-stack capability, proven ownership, and a deliberate move toward broader technical leadership."* Build the real equivalent from the candidate's actual background — don't reuse this example verbatim.
